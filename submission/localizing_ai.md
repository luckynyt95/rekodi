# Localizing AI — a first-person draft (adapt freely)

Localizing AI, to me, is not translating an English app into Swahili. It is
starting from the nurse's reality and refusing to make her come to the technology.

My nurse speaks Swahili first. She thinks in Swahili, she comforts patients in
Swahili, and her clinic notes — when she has time to write them — are in Swahili.
An AI that only works in English is not a tool for her; it is a tax on her time.
So Rekodi listens in Swahili, drafts in Swahili, and reminds patients in Swahili.
The English labels in the demo exist for the judges. For her, the Swahili comes first.

Localizing also means respecting what she already has: a basic phone, no wifi, a
3G bundle she pays for herself. If the AI needs the cloud, it doesn't exist for
her. Ours runs entirely on her phone — 140 MB of models, side-loaded once — because
"offline-first" is not a feature here, it is the precondition for being usable at all.

It means not pretending the AI knows her medicine. The model never suggests a
diagnosis or a treatment, because a mistranslated dosage in a language the model
barely speaks could harm someone. Instead it does the humble work: write down what
she said, flag what it's unsure about, and let her decide. "Sina uhakika — uliza
mtu" — "not sure, ask a person" — is the most important sentence the AI produces.

And it means her data stays hers. Records live on her phone, go to her ministry's
system when she chooses, and can be wiped if the phone is lost. No foreign server
should hold a rural patient's visit notes by default.

If I scale this beyond Swahili, I won't "add languages". I'll sit with the next
nurse, learn how she talks about a visit, and rebuild the prompts, the templates,
and the confidence thresholds around her words. Localization is a relationship,
not a language pack.
